## Lesson Plan (24 Lessons)

### Unit 1: The Health Record — Foundation & Purpose (L1-4)
1. **"The Health Record: Purpose, Users & Functions"** — Definition of the health record (medical record / patient record / chart): a chronological account of patient care. AHIMA definition: "The primary legal record documenting healthcare services provided." Purpose categories: PRIMARY uses (direct patient care — documentation, communication, continuity of care, treatment planning, patient education) vs SECONDARY uses (non-direct — billing/reimbursement, research, quality improvement, public health, education, policy, legal proceedings). Record ownership: facility owns the physical/electronic record, patient owns the information (right to access, amend, accounting of disclosures). Internal users: physicians, nurses, allied health, HIM, administration, risk management, quality management. External users: insurance/payers, government agencies (CMS, CDC, state health departments), accreditors (TJC, DNV), attorneys, law enforcement, researchers, patients/families. HIPAA minimum necessary standard: access limited to what's needed for the user's role. The business record exception: health records as admissible evidence under hearsay rules
   - Cover: Primary vs secondary purposes diagram, internal vs external users grid, ownership concept SVG, minimum necessary access levels

2. **"Evolution of the Health Record"** — Paper records: manila folders, unit numbering, filing systems (terminal digit, straight numeric, middle digit), chart tracking, loose filing, chart assembly. Problems with paper: availability (only one user at a time), legibility, storage costs, damage risk, retrieval time. Transition to hybrid records: some paper + some electronic, scanning, document imaging. Electronic Health Records (EHR): ONC definition, meaningful use / Promoting Interoperability stages (Stage 1: capture data electronically, Stage 2: advance clinical processes, Stage 3: improved outcomes), HITECH Act (2009, $27 billion incentive program, penalties for non-adoption), certified EHR technology (CEHRT), ONC certification criteria. Personal Health Records (PHR): patient-managed, tethered (linked to provider EHR) vs untethered (standalone), patient portals. Key EHR features: CPOE, clinical decision support, e-prescribing, problem lists, medication lists, allergy lists, documentation templates, results viewing, secure messaging. Interoperability: syntactic (data format), semantic (shared meaning), organizational (governance). Current landscape: >96% of hospitals have certified EHR (2023)
   - Cover: Paper → hybrid → EHR timeline, terminal digit filing example, meaningful use stages, EHR core features grid

3. **"Legal Aspects of the Health Record"** — The health record as a legal document: business record, admissible in court under Federal Rules of Evidence 803(6) business records exception. Legal health record (LHR): the subset of all patient data that the organization defines as its official legal record, must be formally defined by policy (what's in vs what's out — email? audit trails? metadata?), used for disclosure/litigation/ROI. Designated record set (DRS): HIPAA term, broader than LHR, includes medical/billing records + any records used to make decisions about the individual, patient has right to access/amend DRS. LHR vs DRS: LHR = what you produce in court, DRS = what patient can access. Authentication: every entry must identify the author, signature requirements (written, electronic, digital), co-signature requirements (residents, NPs, PAs supervised by physicians). Timely documentation: document as close to event as possible, late entries must be labeled as such. Corrections/amendments: single line through error, date/initial/reason, never obliterate, HIPAA amendment rights (patient can request, facility can deny with reason, must attach amendment or denial). Record tampering: spoliation (destruction of evidence), consequences — adverse inference, sanctions, criminal charges
   - Cover: LHR vs DRS Venn diagram, authentication hierarchy, correction procedure steps, spoliation consequences

4. **"Types of Health Records & Care Settings"** — Inpatient records: hospital, acute care, contents specific to hospital stays (H&P, progress notes, orders, operative reports, discharge summary). Outpatient/ambulatory records: physician office, clinics, problem-oriented (POMR) vs source-oriented (SOMR) organization. Emergency department records: triage documentation, ED-specific forms, transfer documentation (EMTALA requirements). Long-term care records: MDS (Minimum Data Set), RAI (Resident Assessment Instrument), care plans, CMS regulatory requirements, surveyors. Behavioral health records: extra confidentiality (42 CFR Part 2 for substance abuse), psychotherapy notes (separate from medical record), treatment plans. Home health records: OASIS assessment, plan of care (485 form), visit notes. Hospice records: IDG (interdisciplinary group) notes, comfort care focus, bereavement care. Rehabilitation records: functional assessments, FIM (Functional Independence Measure), therapy plans. Ambulatory surgery: pre-op assessment, operative report, post-anesthesia evaluation. Dental records: dental charts, periodontal records, imaging. Record content varies by setting but core principles remain: authentication, timeliness, accuracy, completeness
   - Cover: Care setting comparison table (setting vs unique documents vs regulatory body), POMR vs SOMR organization, MDS/OASIS/FIM assessment tools, 42 CFR Part 2 extra protections

### Unit 2: Clinical Documentation Content (L5-10)
5. **"Administrative & Demographic Data"** — Face sheet / registration record: first page of inpatient record. Required data elements: patient name (legal name, aliases), date of birth, gender, race/ethnicity (OMB standards, self-reported), address, phone, emergency contact, employer, insurance information, SSN (optional, HIPAA doesn't require), medical record number (MRN), account/encounter number, attending physician, admission date/time, admission type (emergency, urgent, elective, newborn), admission source (physician referral, ER, transfer, court/law enforcement). Consent forms: general consent to treatment (conditions of admission), informed consent (specific procedures — risks, benefits, alternatives, right to refuse), advance directives (living will, healthcare power of attorney, PSDA — Patient Self-Determination Act 1990), AMA (against medical advice) forms, consent for ROI (release of information). Patient rights: notice of privacy practices (NPP), patient rights statement (TJC requirement), grievance procedures. UB-04 / CMS-1500: billing forms that pull from administrative data. EMPI (Enterprise Master Patient Index): managing identity across systems, MPI clean-up, duplicate resolution
   - Cover: Face sheet data elements grid, consent type hierarchy, advance directive types, EMPI concept diagram

6. **"History & Physical Examination (H&P)"** — H&P: most critical clinical document, must be completed within 24 hours of admission (CMS CoP, TJC standard). History components: Chief Complaint (CC — in patient's own words, reason for encounter), History of Present Illness (HPI — OLDCARTS: Onset, Location, Duration, Character, Aggravating factors, Relieving factors, Timing, Severity), Review of Systems (ROS — systematic organ-by-organ review: constitutional, eyes, ENT, cardiovascular, respiratory, GI, GU, musculoskeletal, integumentary, neurological, psychiatric, endocrine, hematologic/lymphatic, allergic/immunologic), Past Medical History (PMH — prior illnesses, surgeries, hospitalizations), Family History (FH — hereditary conditions, cancer, diabetes, heart disease in blood relatives), Social History (SH — tobacco, alcohol, drugs, occupation, exercise, diet, sexual history, living situation). Physical Examination: general appearance, vital signs, HEENT, neck, chest/lungs, cardiovascular, abdomen, extremities, neurological, skin. Assessment: diagnosis or differential diagnosis. Plan: treatment plan, orders, follow-up. E/M documentation guidelines: history level (problem focused → comprehensive), exam level, medical decision-making complexity. H&P update: if done within 30 days prior to admission, an update note within 24 hours suffices
   - Cover: H&P components flowchart, HPI OLDCARTS mnemonic SVG, ROS 14 systems grid, E/M documentation levels

7. **"Clinical Orders, Progress Notes & SOAP"** — Physician orders: the legal authorization for treatment, must be authenticated (signature + date), types — admission orders (admit to service, condition, vitals, activity, diet, medications, labs, special instructions = "ADC VAAN DIML" mnemonic), medication orders (drug, dose, route, frequency, PRN reason), diagnostic orders (labs, imaging), therapeutic orders (PT, OT, respiratory therapy), transfer/discharge orders. Verbal/telephone orders: read-back verification, must be authenticated within timeframe (typically 24-48 hours, varies by state/facility), limited to emergent situations. CPOE: computerized provider order entry, reduces transcription errors, built-in clinical decision support (drug-drug interaction alerts, allergy alerts, dosing calculators). Progress notes: daily documentation of patient's condition and response to treatment. SOAP format: Subjective (patient reports, symptoms), Objective (exam findings, test results, vital signs), Assessment (diagnosis, clinical impression), Plan (treatment changes, orders, discharge planning). Interdisciplinary notes: nursing notes, therapy notes, social work, nutrition, chaplain. Narrative vs structured documentation: free text vs templates with dropdowns/checkboxes. Documentation burden: note bloat, copy-paste problems, note integrity
   - Cover: Order type hierarchy, ADC VAAN DIML mnemonic, SOAP note anatomy SVG, CPOE workflow with CDS alerts

8. **"Operative & Procedural Documentation"** — Pre-operative documentation: pre-anesthesia evaluation (within 48 hours), anesthesia plan, informed consent (specific to procedure — risks, benefits, alternatives, anesthesia type, surgeon identity), pre-op checklist (site verification, labs, imaging, NPO status). Operative report: MUST be dictated/documented immediately after surgery (TJC, CMS). Required elements: pre-operative diagnosis, post-operative diagnosis, procedure performed, surgeon(s), anesthesiologist, anesthesia type, findings, specimens removed (pathology), estimated blood loss (EBL), complications, drains/implants, disposition/condition. If surgeon unable to document immediately: a brief operative note must be entered (procedure, findings, EBL, condition). Anesthesia record: continuous record during procedure (vitals q5min, medications, fluids, O2, airway management, events). Post-anesthesia evaluation: within 48 hours after surgery, recovery assessment. Pathology report: gross description, microscopic findings, diagnosis, turnaround time requirements. Other procedural documentation: cardiac catheterization, endoscopy, interventional radiology — each has specific documentation requirements. Recovery room / PACU notes: Aldrete scoring (activity, respiration, circulation, consciousness, oxygen saturation)
   - Cover: Operative report required elements, pre-op documentation checklist, anesthesia record timeline, Aldrete scoring system

9. **"Specialized Clinical Reports"** — Consultation reports: requested by attending physician, specialist's findings/recommendations, must be authenticated and filed timely, cannot duplicate the attending's responsibility. Diagnostic imaging/radiology reports: procedure, clinical indication, technique, findings, impression/conclusion, ACR practice guidelines. Laboratory reports: reference ranges, critical values (panic values — immediate notification required), CLIA (Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments) — waived, moderate complexity, high complexity, point-of-care testing. Nursing documentation: admission assessment, care plans (nursing diagnoses, goals, interventions), medication administration record (MAR), vital signs flow sheet, intake & output (I&O), restraint documentation (specific requirements — clinical justification, time-limited orders, frequent reassessment), fall risk assessment. Therapy documentation: PT/OT/ST — evaluation, treatment plan, daily notes, re-evaluation, discharge summary, functional outcome measures. Social work / case management: psychosocial assessment, discharge planning, resource coordination. Nutrition/dietetics: nutrition screening, assessment, care plan, education. Respiratory therapy: ventilator management, ABG documentation, treatments. Medication reconciliation: at every transition of care (admission, transfer, discharge) — compare current medications to new orders, identify discrepancies
   - Cover: Consultation report flow, CLIA lab complexity levels, nursing documentation types grid, medication reconciliation process at transitions

10. **"Discharge Documentation"** — Discharge summary: required for all inpatient stays >48 hours (shorter stays may use abbreviated form). Required elements (CMS/TJC): reason for admission, significant findings (physical exam, labs, imaging), procedures performed, treatment rendered, condition at discharge, patient/family instructions, medications at discharge (reconciled list), follow-up appointments, attending physician signature. Discharge planning: begins at ADMISSION, interdisciplinary (physician, nursing, case management, social work, patient/family), CMS CoP requires discharge planning evaluation, assess post-discharge needs (home health, SNF, rehab, DME, outpatient services). Discharge disposition codes: home/self-care, home with home health, SNF, rehabilitation, transferred to another acute care facility, AMA (against medical advice), expired, hospice. Discharge instructions: medication instructions (new medications, changed medications, stopped medications), activity restrictions, dietary instructions, wound care, follow-up appointments, warning signs requiring emergency care, patient education materials. Transfer documentation: EMTALA requirements for transfers (medical screening exam, stabilization, appropriate transfer with records), sending facility responsibilities, receiving facility acceptance. Death documentation: death summary, autopsy consent/report, cause of death, death certificate completion (attending physician or medical examiner)
    - Cover: Discharge summary required elements checklist, discharge disposition code grid, discharge planning timeline (admission → discharge), transfer documentation EMTALA requirements

### Unit 3: Documentation Standards & Requirements (L11-14)
11. **"Federal Requirements: CMS Conditions of Participation"** — CMS CoPs: legal requirements for Medicare/Medicaid participation, found in 42 CFR Part 482 (hospitals). Key medical record conditions (§482.24): records must be accurately written, promptly completed, properly filed, retained, accessible. Specific requirements: H&P within 24 hours of admission, operative reports immediately after surgery, verbal orders authenticated within 48 hours, all entries dated/timed/authenticated, discharge summary for all inpatient stays, informed consent documented, advance directive documented. Interpretive guidelines: CMS surveyors use these to assess compliance, more detailed than the CoPs themselves. State Operations Manual (SOM): Appendix A provides hospital survey protocol. CMS CoPs for other settings: skilled nursing facilities (42 CFR Part 483 — RAI/MDS requirements, care planning, physician visit schedules), home health agencies (42 CFR Part 484 — OASIS, plan of care, physician orders every 60 days), ambulatory surgical centers (42 CFR Part 416). State licensure: each state has additional requirements, may be more stringent than CMS. Medicare deemed status: accreditation by approved organization (TJC, DNV, HFAP) can substitute for direct CMS survey. Conditions of Payment vs Conditions of Participation: payment rules (accurate billing) vs quality/safety rules
    - Cover: CMS CoP key medical record requirements, 42 CFR Part 482.24 elements, deemed status flow, CoP vs state licensure comparison

12. **"Accreditation Standards: TJC, DNV & Others"** — The Joint Commission (TJC): founded 1951, largest hospital accreditor (~80% of US hospitals), standards organized in chapters (Record of Care — RC, Information Management — IM, Leadership — LD, Provision of Care — PC). TJC documentation standards: comprehensive medical record, H&P within 24 hours, operative report requirements, authentication, informed consent, discharge planning, medication reconciliation. Survey process: unannounced (within 18-month window of 3-year cycle), tracer methodology (individual patient tracer — follows one patient's care through the system, system tracer — evaluates systems like medication management, infection prevention, data management), scoring: requirements + elements of performance (EPs), findings categorized by risk level. DNV GL Healthcare: alternative accreditor, uses ISO 9001 quality management framework, annual surveys (vs TJC 3-year cycle), emphasis on continuous improvement, NIAHO standards (National Integrated Accreditation for Healthcare Organizations). Other accreditors: CARF (rehabilitation), AAAHC (ambulatory care/ASCs), COA (Council on Accreditation), Magnet (nursing excellence — ANCC). NCQA: health plan accreditation, HEDIS measures, patient-centered medical home (PCMH) recognition. Preparing for survey: mock surveys, tracer practice, staff education, documentation compliance audits
    - Cover: TJC vs DNV comparison table, tracer methodology diagram, survey preparation checklist, accreditation body by care setting

13. **"Documentation Integrity & Authorship"** — Authentication: confirming authorship and accuracy of an entry. Methods: written signature (name, credential, date, time), electronic signature (unique identifier, cannot be shared, non-repudiation), digital signature (cryptographic, PKI — public key infrastructure). Auto-authentication: prohibited by CMS — author cannot sign records before they're created, must review before signing. Co-signature requirements: state-dependent, typically required for: resident/intern notes (attending must review and attest), NP/PA notes (supervising physician per state law), verbal/telephone orders, student documentation. Timeliness of authentication: varies by regulation/accreditor/facility policy, typically 24-48 hours for orders, 30 days for H&P completion. Amendments vs corrections vs addenda: Correction = fixing an error (draw single line, date, initial, correct entry), Amendment = patient-requested change to the record (HIPAA right, facility may approve or deny, must attach amendment or denial statement), Addendum = adding information after original entry is complete (clearly labeled with date/time of addendum and reference to original entry). Late entries: document as soon as possible, clearly label as "late entry" with current date/time and reference date. Record completion: deficiency analysis, quantitative analysis (completeness), qualitative analysis (quality/consistency), delinquent records (typically >30 days incomplete), medical staff bylaws define completion requirements, privilege suspension for delinquent records
    - Cover: Authentication methods hierarchy, amendment vs correction vs addendum, deficiency analysis workflow, record completion timeline

14. **"Quantitative & Qualitative Analysis"** — Quantitative analysis (record review): checking the health record for COMPLETENESS — are all required documents present and authenticated? Open-record review: done while patient is still in care (concurrent). Closed-record review: done after discharge (retrospective). Checklist items: H&P present and within 24 hours? All orders authenticated? Operative report present and timely? Consents signed? Discharge summary complete? All entries authored/dated/timed? No blanks in required fields? Deficiency management: deficiency = missing/incomplete item, deficiency slip or electronic notification to responsible provider, suspension policy (privileges suspended if deficiencies not corrected within timeframe — typically 14-30 days). Qualitative analysis: evaluating the QUALITY and CONSISTENCY of documentation — does it make sense clinically? Are diagnoses consistent with diagnostic evidence? Does the plan match the assessment? Are there contradictions? CDI (Clinical Documentation Integrity) connection: qualitative analysis identifies documentation improvement opportunities. Concurrent vs retrospective review: advantages/disadvantages. Record completion rates: compliance metrics, reporting to medical staff committees. HIM department's role: managing the analysis process, tracking deficiencies, reporting to administration, medical staff credentialing implications
    - Cover: Quantitative vs qualitative analysis comparison, deficiency management workflow, record completion metrics dashboard, concurrent vs retrospective review comparison

### Unit 4: Health Data Systems & Standards (L15-18)
15. **"Data Sets & Minimum Data Requirements"** — What is a data set: minimum set of data elements with uniform definitions for a specific purpose. UHDDS (Uniform Hospital Discharge Data Set): inpatient, mandated since 1974 for Medicare/Medicaid, elements include: personal identification, date of birth, sex, race/ethnicity, residence, hospital identification, admission/discharge dates, attending physician, operating physician, principal diagnosis, other diagnoses, qualifier for each diagnosis (present on admission), principal procedure, other procedures, procedure dates, disposition at discharge, expected source of payment. UACDS (Uniform Ambulatory Care Data Set): outpatient, elements include: provider identification, place of encounter, reason for encounter, diagnostic services, problems/diagnoses, therapeutic services, preventive services, disposition, patient profile. MDS (Minimum Data Set): long-term care, part of RAI (Resident Assessment Instrument), comprehensive assessment of functional capabilities, triggers care planning, submitted to CMS, used for quality measures and reimbursement (RUG classification). OASIS (Outcome and Assessment Information Set): home health, CMS-mandated, comprehensive patient assessment, used for outcome measurement and reimbursement (PDGM — Patient-Driven Groupings Model). DEEDS (Data Elements for Emergency Departments). National Vital Statistics System: birth certificates, death certificates
    - Cover: Data set comparison table (UHDDS/UACDS/MDS/OASIS), UHDDS key elements, MDS/RAI/RUG relationship, vital statistics data flow

16. **"Health Data Standards & Vocabularies"** — Why standards matter: interoperability, data exchange, consistent meaning. Vocabulary/terminology standards: ICD-10-CM (diagnosis classification), ICD-10-PCS (inpatient procedure classification), CPT (outpatient procedure codes), HCPCS (supplies, equipment, drugs), SNOMED CT (comprehensive clinical terminology — 350,000+ concepts, reference terminology, maps to ICD/CPT), LOINC (Logical Observation Identifiers Names and Codes — lab tests, clinical measurements, 90,000+ terms), RxNorm (standardized medication names), NDC (National Drug Code — manufacturer, product, package). Transaction/messaging standards: HL7 v2 (ADT messages, orders, results — pipe-delimited segments: MSH|PID|OBR|OBX), HL7 FHIR (Fast Healthcare Interoperability Resources — RESTful API, JSON/XML, resources like Patient, Encounter, Observation), X12 (EDI standard for claims — 837, eligibility — 270/271, remittance — 835), DICOM (medical imaging). Content/document standards: HL7 CDA (Clinical Document Architecture), C-CDA (Consolidated CDA — continuity of care document/CCD, discharge summary, referral note). Privacy/security standards: HIPAA transaction standards. Standard development organizations: HL7 International, SNOMED International, AMA (CPT), WHO (ICD), Regenstrief Institute (LOINC)
    - Cover: Standards layer model (vocabulary → messaging → document → privacy), ICD/CPT/SNOMED/LOINC comparison, HL7 v2 message anatomy, FHIR resource concept

17. **"Health Information Exchange (HIE)"** — HIE defined: electronic sharing of health information between organizations according to nationally recognized standards. Why HIE matters: reduces duplicate tests, improves care coordination, supports public health reporting, reduces medical errors (medication lists, allergy information available across settings). HIE architectures: Directed exchange (point-to-point, like secure email, provider-to-provider), Query-based exchange (pull model, provider requests information from another organization, used for unplanned care), Consumer-mediated exchange (patient controls/accesses their own data, patient portals, PHR). Technical models: centralized (data stored in central repository), federated/distributed (data stays at source, shared on request via record locator service), hybrid. TEFCA (Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement): ONC-led national interoperability framework, QHINs (Qualified Health Information Networks), designated by Sequoia Project/RCE, goal = nationwide interoperability without point-to-point agreements. State HIEs: vary widely in maturity and participation, opt-in vs opt-out consent models. Consent management: patient must consent to participate, HIPAA considerations, state laws may be more restrictive. Public health reporting via HIE: electronic lab reporting (ELR), immunization registries (IIS), syndromic surveillance, cancer reporting. Challenges: sustainability/funding, provider adoption, patient matching across organizations, semantic interoperability, data governance
    - Cover: HIE architecture types (directed/query/consumer), TEFCA/QHIN framework, centralized vs federated model, consent model comparison

18. **"EHR Functions, Design & Implementation"** — EHR functional requirements: ONC certification criteria, USHIK (US Health Information Knowledgebase), core EHR functions — patient demographics, problem lists, medication lists, allergy lists, clinical notes, CPOE, e-prescribing, clinical decision support (CDS), results management, immunization tracking, growth charts, smoking status. CDS types: drug-drug interaction alerts, drug-allergy alerts, dose range checking, clinical guidelines, order sets, reminder systems, predictive analytics. EHR usability: user interface design, workflow integration, alert fatigue (too many alerts → clinicians override), documentation burden, clinician burnout. EHR implementation: system selection (RFP process, vendor evaluation, site visits), planning (governance structure, project management, scope, timeline, budget), build/configuration (data dictionary, templates, order sets, CDS rules, user roles/access), testing (unit, integration, end-to-end, parallel processing), training (super users, classroom, at-the-elbow support), go-live (big bang vs phased), optimization (ongoing improvements). EHR governance: steering committee, clinical informaticists, IT, HIM involvement (data quality, coding integration, CDI). Common EHR vendors: Epic, Oracle Health (Cerner), MEDITECH, Allscripts/Veradigm. Patient portals: ONC 21st Century Cures Act information blocking rules, patient right to electronic access
    - Cover: EHR core functions grid, CDS alert types, implementation lifecycle phases, information blocking rules concept

### Unit 5: Registries, Indices & Data Management (L19-22)
19. **"Master Patient Index (MPI) & Patient Identity"** — MPI defined: the permanent record identifying every patient who has received care at a facility, the most critical index in healthcare. MPI data elements: internal patient ID (MRN), patient name, date of birth, gender, SSN (if collected), address, phone, dates of encounter/admission/discharge, attending physician, health record number. MPI functions: patient identification, record retrieval, duplicate management, record linkage across encounters. Enterprise MPI (EMPI): links patient records across multiple facilities/systems within an enterprise, critical for integrated delivery systems. MPI integrity: duplicate records (same patient → two MRNs), overlays (two patients merged into one MRN — DANGEROUS), integrity issues cause: patient safety risks (wrong information, wrong patient), billing problems, quality data errors, compliance issues. MPI clean-up: identifying duplicates (algorithms: deterministic matching — exact field match, probabilistic matching — statistical likelihood based on weighted fields), manual review, merge/unmerge procedures, ongoing monitoring. Best practices: capture data accurately at registration, standardize name formats (legal name, Jr/Sr, hyphenated), address standardization (USPS format), biometric identification (palm vein scanning, iris recognition), health information exchange patient matching challenges. AHIMA MPI recommendations and data quality standards
    - Cover: MPI data elements, duplicate vs overlay diagram, deterministic vs probabilistic matching, EMPI concept across facilities

20. **"Disease & Procedure Indices and Clinical Registries"** — Indices: organized lists that serve as a reference or guide for locating information. Disease index: cases organized by diagnosis code, enables retrieval of cases by disease, used for research, quality studies, physician queries, public health. Procedure index: cases organized by procedure code, supports surgical quality review, research, utilization analysis. Physician index: cases organized by attending/operating physician, supports credentialing, practice pattern analysis, reappointment. Number index: cross-reference between patient name and health record number. Operation index: links specific surgical procedures to patients. How indices are created: coded data from discharge processing, abstracting (extracting key data elements from the record after discharge), encoder software, automated from EHR. Clinical registries: systematic collection of data for patients with specific diagnoses, conditions, or procedures. Cancer registry: most common, Commission on Cancer (CoC) program, American College of Surgeons (ACS), cancer registrar role (CTR credential), case finding (identifying all cancer cases — disease index, pathology reports, logs), abstracting (demographics, cancer identification, staging — AJCC TNM, treatment, follow-up), NAACCR standards (data standards for cancer registries), SEER program (NCI), state reporting requirements. Trauma registry: NTDB, ACS trauma center verification, ISS/AIS scoring, trauma performance improvement. Other registries: birth defects, immunization (IIS), transplant (UNOS/OPTN), cardiac (STS), implant, diabetes
    - Cover: Index type comparison, cancer registry workflow (case finding → abstracting → staging → follow-up), TNM staging concept, registry type comparison grid

21. **"Record Retention, Storage & Destruction"** — Retention defined: how long health records must be kept. No single federal law dictates retention for all records. Retention sources: state statutes (vary widely — 5-10+ years for adults, typically until age of majority + statute of limitations for minors), federal requirements (Medicare CoPs don't specify exact retention, but §482.24 requires policies), accreditation standards, state licensure, malpractice statute of limitations (varies by state — typically 2-3 years from discovery, longer for minors), professional guidelines (AHIMA recommends: 10 years after most recent encounter for adults, age of majority + statute of limitations for minors). Retention schedule: facility-specific policy document, lists record types and retention periods, approved by legal counsel, HIM, administration, compliance. Storage methods: on-site/active file area (most recent records), off-site storage (contracted, environmental controls, security), microfilm/microfiche (legacy, still legally valid, 16mm/35mm roll, aperture cards, computer-output microfilm/COM), optical disc imaging (scanning, document management systems), cloud storage (vendor agreements, BAAs, data security, disaster recovery). Destruction methods: shredding (paper, cross-cut preferred), degaussing (magnetic media), overwriting (electronic), physical destruction (burning, pulverizing), certificate of destruction (document what was destroyed, method, date, witnesses). Legal holds: stop destruction if litigation is pending or reasonably anticipated, failure to hold = spoliation. EHR retention considerations: metadata, audit trails, system migration, vendor contracts
    - Cover: Retention decision flowchart (federal → state → accreditation → policy), storage method comparison, destruction methods and when to use each, legal hold process

22. **"Forms Design & Management"** — Why forms matter: standardize data collection, ensure completeness, support workflow, legal compliance. Forms design principles: purpose-driven (what data needs to be captured?), user-centered (who will complete it?), standardized (consistent layout), compliant (regulatory requirements). Design elements: facility identification (name, address, logo), form title and number (unique identifier), patient identification area (typically top of every page — name, MRN, DOB, account number), instructions (clear, concise), data capture areas (checkboxes, text fields, dropdown menus), margins (for binding, scanning — 3/4" left margin for hole-punched charts), bar codes (for document imaging, chart tracking), signature/date lines, version control (revision date, form number). Paper vs electronic forms: EHR templates, structured data capture vs free text, dropdown menus, auto-population of demographics, forcing functions (required fields), clinical decision support integration. Forms committee: interdisciplinary (HIM, medical staff, nursing, IT, legal, administration), reviews all new/revised forms, approval process, ensures no duplication, standardization across facility. Forms inventory: catalog of all active forms, regular review cycle, obsolete form removal. Screen design for EHR: 15-inch rule (most critical info visible without scrolling), minimize clicks, logical workflow order, specialty-specific views
    - Cover: Form design elements labeled example, paper form requirements checklist, forms committee workflow, EHR template design principles

### Unit 6: Data Governance & Capstone (L23-24)
23. **"Health Data Governance & Stewardship"** — Data governance: enterprise-wide framework for managing data assets — availability, usability, integrity, security. Data governance structure: executive sponsor, data governance council (cross-functional), data stewards (domain-specific custodians), data analysts, IT. Key functions: data quality management (accuracy, completeness, consistency, timeliness, validity), data dictionary/metadata management (defining every data element — name, definition, format, allowable values, source system, owner), data standards enforcement, data access management (who can see/use what data), data lifecycle management (creation → use → retention → disposition). Data quality dimensions: accuracy (does it reflect reality?), completeness (are all required fields populated?), consistency (same data in different systems match?), timeliness (is it current?), validity (does it conform to allowable values/format?), uniqueness (no duplicates?). HIM role in data governance: data quality experts, coding accuracy, CDI, MPI integrity, ROI compliance, registry data quality, documentation standards. Master data management (MDM): single source of truth for key entities (patients, providers, locations, payers). Data mapping: translating between code sets/vocabularies, crosswalks (ICD-9 to ICD-10 General Equivalence Mappings), terminology mapping. Information governance: broader than data governance — encompasses records management, privacy/security, legal compliance, data analytics strategy
    - Cover: Data governance framework pyramid, data quality dimensions hexagon, data steward responsibilities, information governance vs data governance
