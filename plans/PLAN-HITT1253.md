## Lesson Plan (22 Lessons)

### Unit 1: Legal Foundations (L1-4)
1. **"The American Legal System & Healthcare"** — Sources of law: constitutional (federal/state, 14th Amendment due process/equal protection), statutory (legislation, Public Health Service Act, Social Security Act), administrative/regulatory (CFR — 42 CFR for CMS, 45 CFR for HIPAA, FDA regulations), case law/common law (judicial decisions, precedent/stare decisis). Federal vs state jurisdiction: federal preemption, HIPAA floor (state law applies if more restrictive). Court system: federal (district → circuit → Supreme Court) vs state (trial → appellate → supreme). How a law becomes a regulation: statute → proposed rule (NPRM) → public comment → final rule → CFR. Key regulatory agencies: HHS, CMS, ONC, OCR, OIG, FDA, FTC, state health departments
   - Cover: Court system hierarchy SVG, law-to-regulation flowchart, federal vs state jurisdiction diagram

2. **"Tort Law, Negligence & Medical Malpractice"** — Tort: civil wrong causing harm, intentional vs unintentional. Negligence: duty, breach, causation, damages (the 4 Ds: Duty, Dereliction, Direct cause, Damages). Medical malpractice: professional negligence, standard of care (what a reasonable professional would do), expert witness requirement. Specific torts: battery (unauthorized touching/treatment), assault (threat), false imprisonment (restraints without justification), defamation (libel/slander about patient), invasion of privacy (PHI disclosure), fraud, intentional infliction of emotional distress. Res ipsa loquitur: "the thing speaks for itself" — shifts burden of proof (sponge left in surgical site). Respondeat superior: employer liable for employee actions within scope. Corporate negligence: Darling v. Charleston Community Memorial Hospital (1965) — hospital liable for failing to supervise medical staff. Statute of limitations: varies by state, typically 2-3 years, discovery rule, tolling for minors. Damage caps: state variations
   - Cover: 4 Ds of negligence diagram, tort classification table, Darling case summary

3. **"Informed Consent & Patient Rights"** — Informed consent: legal doctrine (patient has right to make decisions about their own body), 5 elements (nature of treatment, risks, benefits, alternatives, consequences of refusal), who can give consent (competent adult, legal guardian, emancipated minor), exceptions (emergency, therapeutic privilege, patient waiver). Implied consent: emergency treatment, routine procedures (venipuncture). Capacity vs competency: capacity = clinical determination, competency = legal determination by court. Right to refuse treatment: competent adults can refuse any treatment including life-saving, Cruzan v. Director (1990) — clear and convincing evidence standard. AHA Patient Care Partnership (replaced Patient Bill of Rights). Advance directives: living will, durable power of attorney for healthcare (DPAHC), DNR/DNAR, POLST/MOLST (physician orders, portable across settings). Patient Self-Determination Act (1990): hospitals must inform patients of AD rights at admission. Minors: age of majority (18 in most states), emancipated minors, mature minor doctrine, minor consent exceptions (STD, substance abuse, mental health, pregnancy — varies by state)
   - Cover: 5 elements of informed consent SVG, advance directive comparison table, minor consent decision tree

4. **"Medical Records as Legal Documents"** — Health record as business record exception to hearsay: Federal Rules of Evidence 803(6), foundation requirements (made at/near time of event, by person with knowledge, kept in regular course of business). Authentication: who can document (licensed/credentialed individuals), signature requirements, electronic signature (ESIGN Act, state laws), auto-authentication concerns. Amendments, corrections, addenda: correction (contemporaneous fix, single line through error, initials/date/reason), late entry (documentation added after normal timeframe), amendment (request to change information patient believes is incorrect — HIPAA right), addenda (supplemental information). Record retention: no single federal law (HIPAA requires 6 years for administrative records, CMS 5 years for Medicare records, state laws vary widely — AHIMA recommends 10 years adult, age of majority + statute of limitations for minors). Destruction: shredding, pulping, degaussing, overwriting, burning; certificate of destruction; no destruction during litigation hold. Legal holds: obligation to preserve records when litigation is reasonably anticipated. E-discovery: electronically stored information (ESI), metadata preservation
   - Cover: Correction vs amendment vs addenda comparison, retention schedule table, destruction methods

### Unit 2: HIPAA Privacy (L5-9)
5. **"HIPAA Overview & Administrative Simplification"** — HIPAA (1996): Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act, P.L. 104-191. Title I: insurance portability (pre-existing conditions, COBRA). Title II: Administrative Simplification — 5 rules: Privacy Rule (45 CFR 164 Subpart E), Security Rule (45 CFR 164 Subpart C), Transactions & Code Sets Rule (45 CFR 162), Unique Identifiers Rule (NPI), Enforcement Rule. HITECH Act (2009): strengthened HIPAA enforcement, extended to business associates, mandatory breach notification, increased penalties, meaningful use. Covered entities: health plans, healthcare clearinghouses, healthcare providers who transmit electronically. Business associates: definition (performs function involving PHI on behalf of CE), examples (billing companies, law firms, cloud storage, shredding companies, EHR vendors, accountants), BA of BA (subcontractor). PHI: individually identifiable health information, any form (paper, electronic, oral), relates to past/present/future health condition, provision of care, or payment. 18 HIPAA identifiers (LIST ALL 18 — names, dates, phone, fax, email, SSN, MRN, health plan beneficiary, account numbers, certificate/license, VIN/serial, device IDs, URLs, IPs, biometric, full-face photos, any other unique number)
   - Cover: HIPAA title structure SVG, covered entity decision tree, 18 identifiers grid

6. **"HIPAA Privacy Rule: Patient Rights"** — Right of access (45 CFR 164.524): access to PHI in designated record set, 30 days to respond (+30 day extension), format requested if readily producible, reasonable cost-based fee, cannot deny access to own records (limited exceptions: psychotherapy notes, information compiled for legal proceedings, certain lab results under CLIA, inmates), HIPAA Right of Access Initiative (OCR enforcement priority). Right to amendment (45 CFR 164.526): 60 days to respond (+30 day extension), may deny if: CE didn't create it, not part of DRS, accurate and complete, not available for access. Right to accounting of disclosures (45 CFR 164.528): 6-year lookback, excludes: TPO, to the individual, incidental, national security, correctional institutions, authorized disclosures. Right to request restrictions (45 CFR 164.522): CE generally not required to agree EXCEPT: must restrict if individual pays out of pocket in full and disclosure is to health plan for payment/operations. Right to confidential communications (45 CFR 164.522): reasonable request for alternative means/location, health plans must accommodate. Right to notice (45 CFR 164.520): Notice of Privacy Practices (NPP), must be provided at first service, posted in facility, on website
   - Cover: Patient rights summary table with timelines, access denial exceptions, restriction exception diagram

7. **"HIPAA Privacy Rule: Uses, Disclosures & Authorizations"** — Uses vs disclosures: use = within CE, disclosure = to outside entity. TPO: Treatment (providers sharing for care), Payment (claims, eligibility, UR, collections), Operations (QA, auditing, legal, business management, accreditation). Minimum necessary: applies to ALL uses/disclosures EXCEPT: treatment, patient requests, authorizations, DHHS investigations, required by law, compliance with Privacy Rule. Authorizations: required elements (description of PHI, who authorized, who receives, purpose, expiration, signature/date) + required statements (right to revoke, impact on treatment/payment/enrollment, potential for redisclosure). Authorization ALWAYS required for: psychotherapy notes, marketing, sale of PHI. Permitted uses/disclosures WITHOUT authorization (12 categories): required by law, public health, abuse/neglect/domestic violence, health oversight, judicial/administrative proceedings, law enforcement (6 specific circumstances), decedents, organ/tissue donation, research (3 exceptions: waiver of authorization, preparatory to research, decedent research), serious threat, essential government functions, workers' compensation. De-identification: Safe Harbor (remove all 18 identifiers + no actual knowledge) vs Expert Determination (statistical/scientific determination), limited data set (some identifiers remain, requires DUA)
   - Cover: TPO definition boxes, authorization elements checklist, 12 permitted disclosures grid, de-identification comparison

8. **"Release of Information (ROI) Operations"** — ROI process: receive request → determine type (authorization, subpoena, court order, permitted disclosure, patient request) → validate request → verify identity/authority → determine scope → retrieve records → review for protected content (psychotherapy notes, HIV in some states, substance abuse/42 CFR Part 2, mental health, genetic information/GINA) → prepare response → track in disclosure log → invoice if applicable. Authorization validation: check all 6 elements + 3 statements, check expiration, check for revocation, check scope. Legal process: subpoena (attorney-issued, requires satisfactory assurance — notice to patient OR qualified protective order), court order (judge-signed, comply within scope), warrant (law enforcement, immediate compliance). Turnaround times: HIPAA 30 days for patient access, state laws may require faster. Fees: HIPAA right of access (reasonable cost-based: labor for copying, supplies, postage, prep of summary if agreed upon), state fee schedules. Denial of access: reasons, appeal process. Third-party requests: personal representatives, attorneys, insurance companies, employers. Redisclosure notice: "This information has been disclosed to you from records protected by federal confidentiality rules"
   - Cover: ROI workflow flowchart, request type decision tree, subpoena vs court order vs warrant comparison

9. **"Special Privacy Protections & State Law"** — 42 CFR Part 2 (Substance Abuse): MORE restrictive than HIPAA, applies to federally assisted programs, specific written consent required (10 elements), prohibition on redisclosure, qualified service organization agreement (QSOA, equivalent of BAA), 2024 SAMHSA/HHS final rule aligning with HIPAA. State HIV/AIDS confidentiality laws: many states have specific consent requirements, partner notification, anonymous testing. Mental health records: state-specific protections, psychotherapy notes under HIPAA (always requires authorization, separate from medical record). Genetic information: GINA (Genetic Information Nondiscrimination Act 2008) — prohibits genetic discrimination in employment and health insurance, HIPAA treats genetic information as PHI. Minors: state law governs consent and access, parents generally have access UNLESS minor consented to treatment (varies widely). FERPA (student health records at schools) vs HIPAA — which applies? State preemption analysis: HIPAA is the floor, if state law is MORE protective/provides MORE rights, state law applies. Practical preemption scenarios
   - Cover: 42 CFR Part 2 vs HIPAA comparison table, state law preemption decision tree, special population protections grid

### Unit 3: HIPAA Security & Breach (L10-12)
10. **"HIPAA Security Rule"** — Scope: ePHI only (not paper or oral). Three safeguard categories with ALL specifications: **Administrative** (12 standards): security management process (risk analysis, risk management, sanction policy, information system activity review), assigned security responsibility (security officer), workforce security (authorization/supervision, workforce clearance, termination procedures), information access management (access authorization, access establishment/modification), security awareness training (security reminders, protection from malicious software, log-in monitoring, password management), security incident procedures, contingency plan (data backup, disaster recovery, emergency mode operations, testing, application/data criticality analysis), evaluation, BAAs. **Physical** (4 standards): facility access controls (contingency operations, facility security plan, access control/validation, maintenance records), workstation use, workstation security, device/media controls (disposal, media re-use, accountability, data backup/storage). **Technical** (5 standards): access control (unique user identification, emergency access procedure, automatic logoff, encryption/decryption), audit controls, integrity (mechanism to authenticate ePHI), person/entity authentication, transmission security (integrity controls, encryption). Required vs Addressable: addressable ≠ optional — must implement, implement alternative, or document why neither is reasonable
   - Cover: Three safeguards with all standards SVG (large reference card), required vs addressable explanation, risk analysis steps

11. **"Breach Notification Rule & Enforcement"** — Breach definition: unauthorized acquisition, access, use, or disclosure of UNSECURED PHI that compromises security or privacy. Unsecured PHI: not rendered unusable/unreadable/indecipherable (encryption, destruction). 4-factor risk assessment to determine if breach occurred: nature/extent of PHI involved, unauthorized person who received, whether PHI was actually acquired/viewed, extent of mitigation. 3 exceptions (NOT a breach): unintentional access by workforce member acting in good faith within scope, inadvertent disclosure between authorized persons at same CE/BA, good faith belief that unauthorized person couldn't retain information. Notification requirements: to individuals (within 60 days of DISCOVERY — not occurrence, first-class mail or email if agreed, substitute notice for insufficient contact info), to HHS (500+ = within 60 days + notify prominent media outlet in state, <500 = annual log due within 60 days of calendar year end), to media (if 500+ in a state/jurisdiction). HHS breach portal ("Wall of Shame"). HITECH enforcement: 4 penalty tiers (Tier 1 unknowing $137-$68,928, Tier 2 reasonable cause $1,379-$68,928, Tier 3 willful neglect corrected $13,785-$68,928, Tier 4 willful neglect not corrected $68,928-$2,067,813 per year), criminal penalties (DOJ), state AG authority to bring actions. OCR enforcement process: complaint → investigation → resolution (technical assistance, resolution agreement, CMP)
   - Cover: Breach assessment flowchart, notification timeline diagram, penalty tier table, OCR enforcement process

12. **"Cybersecurity & Information Security in Practice"** — Threats: ransomware (healthcare #1 target industry, $10.1M average breach cost), phishing/social engineering, insider threats (curiosity, malice), malware, DDoS, supply chain attacks. NIST Cybersecurity Framework: 5 functions (Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover) — map to HIPAA Security Rule. Risk analysis process: identify ePHI, identify threats/vulnerabilities, assess current controls, determine likelihood/impact, assign risk level, implement mitigation, document everything. Access controls in practice: role-based access (RBAC), minimum necessary applied to system access, user provisioning/de-provisioning, multi-factor authentication, privileged access management. Incident response plan: detection → containment → eradication → recovery → lessons learned. Workforce training: annual HIPAA training, phishing simulations, social engineering awareness, reporting procedures. Emerging threats: cloud security (shared responsibility model), mobile device management (MDM), IoT medical devices, telehealth security. HIM-specific security: coding remotely (VPN, encrypted connections), ROI transmission security, EHR audit log monitoring
   - Cover: NIST CSF five functions diagram, risk analysis matrix, incident response steps, threat landscape infographic

### Unit 4: Healthcare Law & Regulations (L13-17)
13. **"Fraud & Abuse Laws"** — False Claims Act (31 USC 3729-3733): civil, "knowingly" standard (actual knowledge, deliberate ignorance, reckless disregard — no specific intent required), treble damages + $13,946-$27,894 per claim, qui tam/whistleblower provisions (15-30% of recovery, anti-retaliation protections), responsible for both submitting false claims AND retaining overpayments (60-day repayment rule). Anti-Kickback Statute (42 USC 1320a-7b): CRIMINAL, any remuneration to induce/reward referrals for items/services paid by federal healthcare programs, "one purpose" test (if even one purpose is to induce referrals = violation), penalties ($100K + 10 years per offense + exclusion + treble damages), safe harbors (26 safe harbors including: employee, personal services, space/equipment rental, investment in ASCs/group practices, EHR donations). Stark Law (42 USC 1395nn): CIVIL strict liability (no intent required), physician self-referral prohibition for designated health services (DHS — 12 services including clinical lab, imaging, DME, home health, inpatient/outpatient hospital, outpatient Rx, PT/OT/SLP), exceptions (in-office ancillary, physician services, fair market value, academic medical centers). Key distinctions: AKS = criminal + intent, Stark = civil + strict liability, FCA = civil + knowing standard
   - Cover: Three fraud laws comparison table (penalties, intent, scope), safe harbor examples, DHS list, qui tam process

14. **"EMTALA & Patient Access Laws"** — EMTALA (1986): Emergency Medical Treatment and Active Labor Act, applies to ALL hospitals with EDs that accept Medicare (virtually all), two requirements: Medical Screening Examination (MSE — appropriate within hospital's capability, not just triage), stabilization (treatment to prevent material deterioration) OR appropriate transfer (risks vs benefits, receiving hospital accepts, medical records transferred, qualified personnel/equipment during transport). Applies to ALL patients regardless of ability to pay. Penalties: $119,942 per violation (physician and hospital), Medicare termination. "250-yard rule": applies to hospital campus. On-call physicians must respond. ADA: Americans with Disabilities Act — reasonable accommodations, accessible facilities, auxiliary aids for communication (interpreters, TTY), service animals. Section 504 Rehabilitation Act: no discrimination based on disability in federally funded programs. Title VI Civil Rights Act: no discrimination based on race/color/national origin, LEP (Limited English Proficiency) requirements — meaningful access to services, qualified interpreters. Section 1557 ACA: consolidated nondiscrimination (race, color, national origin, sex, age, disability). Hill-Burton obligations: free/reduced care for communities
   - Cover: EMTALA two-part requirement SVG, transfer requirements checklist, patient access law comparison table

15. **"Employment Law in Healthcare"** — Title VII Civil Rights Act (1964): prohibits discrimination based on race, color, religion, sex, national origin; applies to employers with 15+ employees; EEOC enforcement; sexual harassment (quid pro quo, hostile work environment). ADA (employment): reasonable accommodation, essential job functions, interactive process, undue hardship defense. ADEA: Age Discrimination in Employment Act, 40+ protected, 20+ employees. FMLA: Family and Medical Leave Act, 12 weeks unpaid leave per year, 50+ employees within 75 miles, serious health condition, birth/adoption/foster, qualifying exigency, military caregiver (26 weeks). Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA): minimum wage, overtime (1.5× for >40 hours/week), exempt vs non-exempt (salary level test $35,568, salary basis test, duties test — executive/administrative/professional/computer), child labor protections. OSHA: Occupational Safety and Health Act, workplace safety, bloodborne pathogen standard, TB exposure control, violence prevention, HIM ergonomics. Workers' compensation: state-mandated, no-fault, covers medical expenses + lost wages for work-related injury/illness. At-will employment: employer or employee can terminate for any reason (except illegal ones), exceptions (public policy, implied contract, covenant of good faith). Wrongful termination: retaliation, discrimination, whistleblower protection
   - Cover: Protected class comparison table, FMLA eligibility flowchart, exempt vs non-exempt decision tree

16. **"Health Information-Specific Legislation"** — HITECH Act (2009): EHR incentives ($27B+), Meaningful Use → Promoting Interoperability, HIPAA enforcement strengthened (BA directly liable, breach notification mandate, increased penalties, state AG authority, willful neglect mandatory investigation). 21st Century Cures Act (2016): information blocking prohibition (8 exceptions: preventing harm, privacy, security, infeasibility, health IT performance, content/manner, fees, licensing), TEFCA (Trusted Exchange Framework and Common Agreement), USCDI (United States Core Data for Interoperability), patient access to EHI via APIs. No Surprises Act (2022): protect against surprise out-of-network bills, IDR (Independent Dispute Resolution) process, good faith estimates for uninsured/self-pay, provider directory accuracy, continuity of care. Price Transparency: Hospital Price Transparency Rule (machine-readable files, 300 shoppable services), Transparency in Coverage Rule (health plans). GINA (2008): genetic information nondiscrimination. Newborns' and Mothers' Health Protection Act: minimum maternity stays. Mental Health Parity and Addiction Equity Act: equal coverage requirements
   - Cover: HITECH provisions timeline, 21st Century Cures info blocking diagram, No Surprises Act patient protections

17. **"Accreditation, Licensure & Credentialing Law"** — Licensure: mandatory government permission to practice/operate, individual (physicians via state medical board, nurses via state board of nursing, therapists) vs facility (state health department surveys, Life Safety Code, annual renewal). Certification: voluntary recognition of competency by professional organization, RHIT/RHIA (AHIMA), CCS/CPC, board-certified physicians (ABMS). Accreditation: voluntary evaluation by recognized body, deemed status (TJC/DNV accreditation satisfies CMS CoPs — don't need separate CMS survey). The Joint Commission: history (1951 JCAH → 1987 JCAHO → 2007 TJC), unannounced surveys (3-year cycle), tracer methodology (follow the patient), sentinel event policy, National Patient Safety Goals, HIM-relevant standards (record of care, information management). CMS Conditions of Participation: regulatory requirements for Medicare participation, CoPs (hospitals) vs CfCs (other providers), deficiency levels (immediate jeopardy = most serious), state survey agencies. DNV GL: ISO 9001-based, annual surveys. CARF, NCQA, AAAHC. Medical staff credentialing: primary source verification, NPDB (Healthcare Quality Improvement Act 1986 — mandatory reporting), privileging, OPPE/FPPE, peer review protections
   - Cover: Licensure vs certification vs accreditation comparison, TJC survey process, CMS deficiency levels, credentialing workflow

### Unit 5: Ethics in HIM (L18-21)
18. **"Ethical Foundations for HIM Professionals"** — Ethical theories: deontology (Kant — duty-based, categorical imperative, do right regardless of outcome), utilitarianism (Mill — greatest good for greatest number, cost-benefit), virtue ethics (Aristotle — character and moral virtue), rights-based ethics (inherent human rights). Bioethical principles (Beauchamp & Childress): autonomy (self-determination), beneficence (do good), nonmaleficence (do no harm), justice (fairness in resource distribution). Ethical decision-making models: identify problem → gather facts → identify stakeholders → consider options → apply ethical principles → decide → evaluate outcome. Ethics vs law: legal ≠ ethical (can be legal but unethical, or ethical but illegal). Professional codes of ethics: purpose, enforcement, self-regulation. Moral distress: when you know the right action but are constrained from taking it. Cultural competency: respecting diverse values, health literacy, language access
   - Cover: Four bioethical principles diagram, ethical theory comparison table, decision-making model flowchart

19. **"AHIMA Code of Ethics & Coding Ethics"** — AHIMA Code of Ethics (11 principles): advocate for privacy/confidentiality, quality data, professional competency, refuse to participate in illegal activities, advance knowledge, accountability, mentor others, honest representation, maintain coding quality, promote interdisciplinary collaboration, respect self-determination. Enforcement: AHIMA Ethics Committee, sanctions (censure, membership suspension, credential revocation). AHIMA Standards of Ethical Coding: code based on documentation, don't change codes for reimbursement, query for unclear documentation, refuse pressure to code inaccurately, report breaches of coding ethics, maintain coding competency, protect patient information. Coding ethical dilemmas: CFO pressure to upcode, physician asking to add a diagnosis not documented, coder finding a pattern of upcoding by colleague, unbundling requests, "optimization" vs manipulation. Whistleblower protections: False Claims Act qui tam, state whistleblower laws, retaliation prohibitions. Case studies with ethical analysis
   - Cover: 11 AHIMA principles grid, ethical coding standards, dilemma resolution framework

20. **"Bioethics: End-of-Life, Research & Resource Allocation"** — End-of-life: euthanasia (active vs passive, voluntary vs involuntary — illegal in most U.S. jurisdictions), physician-assisted death (PAD, legal in 10+ states, Death with Dignity Acts), palliative care vs hospice (palliative = any stage, hospice = terminal prognosis ≤6 months), withholding vs withdrawing treatment (ethically equivalent), ordinary vs extraordinary means, medical futility. Brain death: Uniform Determination of Death Act (UDDA — irreversible cessation of all brain functions including brainstem). Organ donation: Uniform Anatomical Gift Act, UNOS allocation, opt-in vs opt-out systems, living donors, ethical issues in allocation (justice, utility, medical urgency). Research ethics: Nuremberg Code (10 principles, response to Nazi experiments), Tuskegee syphilis study (1932-1972, led to Belmont Report), Belmont Report (respect for persons, beneficence, justice), IRB (exempt, expedited, full review), informed consent in research, vulnerable populations (children, prisoners, pregnant women, cognitively impaired). Resource allocation: triage ethics (utilitarianism vs egalitarianism), pandemic allocation frameworks, rationing
   - Cover: End-of-life options spectrum, research ethics timeline (Nuremberg → Tuskegee → Belmont), organ allocation factors

21. **"Ethics Committees & Institutional Compliance"** — Ethics committees: composition (physicians, nurses, social workers, ethicists, chaplains, community members, legal counsel), three functions (education, policy development, case consultation), consultation process (who can request, committee vs individual consultant, advisory not binding), common consultation triggers (withdrawal of care, capacity disputes, surrogate decision-making, resource allocation). Institutional compliance: compliance officer role and reporting structure (reports to CEO/Board, independent of operations), compliance committee, compliance program elements (OIG 7 elements), relationship between ethics and compliance. Conflicts of interest: physician self-referral, pharmaceutical relationships, gift policies, Sunshine Act (Open Payments — physician payment transparency). Corporate responsibility: organizational ethics, mission-driven vs profit-driven, community benefit (nonprofit hospital requirements). HIM ethical leadership: data integrity advocacy, standing up against improper coding pressure, maintaining patient confidentiality culture, privacy officer role
   - Cover: Ethics committee consultation flowchart, compliance officer reporting structure, conflict of interest types

### Unit 6: Capstone (L22)
22. **"Capstone: Legal & Ethical Decision-Making in HIM"** — Integrated case studies combining legal AND ethical analysis: Case 1: Celebrity patient — media requests, workforce snooping, breach response, ethical obligations. Case 2: Subpoena for substance abuse records — 42 CFR Part 2 vs HIPAA vs state law analysis. Case 3: Physician pressure to change coding — ethical coding standards, compliance reporting, whistleblower protections, FCA implications. Case 4: Breach discovery — risk assessment, notification decisions, mitigation, OCR investigation response. Case 5: End-of-life dispute — advance directives, ethics committee consultation, surrogate decision-making, documentation requirements. Case 6: Research request for patient data — IRB, waiver of authorization, de-identification, limited data set analysis. For each case: identify legal issues → identify ethical issues → apply relevant laws/regulations → apply ethical principles → determine best course of action → explain rationale. Final reflection: the HIM professional as guardian of patient information — legal and ethical responsibilities
   - Cover: Case documentation excerpts, legal/ethical analysis frameworks, decision trees for each case
